Core points
  • The gyro has no horizon. p, q, r are rotation rates about the rocket's own nose and fin axes. They read the same no matter where the rocket points.
  • World roll is computed, not measured. It is the angle between the green fin and a reference direction (amber) that the world defines. In the horizon convention that reference is "horizontal and perpendicular to the nose". In the vertical convention it is "perpendicular to both the nose and North" (East when the rocket sits on the pad).
  • The two match only when pitch and yaw rates are zero, or when θ = 0. Otherwise q and r feed into world roll rate through the tan θ term. That is why ∫p drifts away from φ.
  • At θ = ±90° world roll is undefined. Horizon convention: nose straight up (on the pad). Vertical convention: nose pointing exactly North or South (the launch frame's crossrange axis). A rocket pitching over toward East never reaches it. The singularity moves when you change convention. The gyro readings do not change.
  • For flight software: integrate a quaternion from p, q, r. Never integrate p and call it roll. Convert to Euler angles only for display, logging and control targets, and use a convention whose singularity is outside the flight envelope. For the boost phase that is vertical-referenced. For a pitched-over trajectory near horizontal it is horizon-referenced.

What each condition does
  • Condition 1 alone leaves a full circle. Any roll is physically possible. On its own, the body gives no preferred fin direction.
  • Condition 2 is the only input from the world. It turns "a fin direction" into "the zero-roll fin direction". Change the axis and you change what zero roll means.
  • 1 + 2 leave one line, perpendicular to both the nose and the axis. It depends only on where the nose sits around the axis, never on how far from it. Moving the nose straight toward the axis does not turn the line. Moving it sideways around the axis does.
  • Condition 3 hides how weakly the line is defined. The raw cross product has length sin(angle to axis). Normalizing divides by that, so near the axis small sideways nose motions turn the amber line by large angles.
  • Condition 4 turns the line into a direction. Switch on the field view: the ticks circle around the two points where the nose lines up with ±axis. Every axis choice has two such points. You can move them but not remove them.
  • Design rule: choose the axis so that both bad points sit where the nose never goes. During boost that means not Up.
